Sprachbausteine: Exercises & Tips for TELC and Goethe

Sprachbausteine are the section most students skip in their exam prep. That is a mistake. These cloze-style tasks test your grammar and vocabulary in context, and they are worth a big chunk of points. Here is everything you need to know to prepare properly.

September 5, 2026 12 min read Level A2 - B2

What you will learn in this guide

  • What Sprachbausteine actually are and why they matter for your exam score.
  • Which exams include Sprachbausteine and at which levels.
  • The two task types: multiple-choice cloze and word bank.

  • Worked example exercises you can try right now.
  • Level-specific tips from A2 to B2.

What are Sprachbausteine?

The word Sprachbausteine translates roughly to "language building blocks." In practice, it refers to a specific section of German language exams where you fill gaps in a text by choosing the correct word or phrase. The text is usually an email, a letter, or a short article, and the gaps test whether you can handle grammar, vocabulary, and collocations in real-world German.

Think of it this way: reading comprehension tests whether you understand a text. Sprachbausteine test whether you can produce the right piece of language when the context demands it. You are not writing freely. You are picking from options. But picking correctly requires solid grammar knowledge and a feel for how German actually works in connected text.

This section sits between reading and writing in most exam formats. It is often called "Language Elements" or "Sprachbausteine" on the exam paper itself. Many students treat it as a minor annoyance. That is a strategic error, because it is one of the sections where careful preparation leads to the biggest score improvements.

Which exams include Sprachbausteine?

Sprachbausteine appear in the two main German exam families:

TELC exams

TELC includes Sprachbausteine at every level from A2 to C1. At B1 and B2, the Sprachbausteine section is a significant part of the reading module. The TELC B1 exam has two Sprachbausteine tasks, each with 10 items. That is 20 items out of the entire reading and language section. If you get them all right, you have essentially secured a passing grade for that module before you even start the composition.

At B2, the tasks become more complex. You deal with longer texts, more nuanced grammar, and vocabulary that goes beyond everyday conversation. But the format is the same, and the strategy is the same.

Goethe-Institut exams

The Goethe B1 exam integrates similar cloze-style tasks into the reading section, although they may not always carry the explicit label "Sprachbausteine." At B2 and C1, Goethe exams include tasks that are functionally identical: a text with gaps, a set of options, and grammar knowledge required to fill them correctly.

The Goethe format tends to be slightly more demanding in terms of vocabulary range, but the underlying grammar points tested are largely the same as TELC.

DTZ and integration exams

The Deutsch-Test fur Zuwanderer (DTZ), which covers A2-B1, also includes Sprachbausteine. If you are preparing for an integration course final exam, this section matters to you too.

The two types of Sprachbausteine tasks

Regardless of the exam provider, Sprachbausteine come in two formats. You need to be comfortable with both.

Type 1: Multiple-choice cloze

You read a text with gaps. For each gap, you get three options (a, b, c). You pick the one that fits grammatically and semantically. This is the more common format, especially at B1.

What makes it tricky: the wrong options are not random. They are designed to catch specific mistakes. One option might have the wrong preposition. Another might have the right preposition but the wrong case. The third is correct. If you do not know the grammar rule, all three look plausible.

Example of what you might see:

GapOption aOption bOption c
Ich interessiere mich ___ Musik.anfuruber
Er hat ___ seiner Frau gesprochen.mitzuvon
Das hangt ___ dem Wetter ab.vonaufan

The correct answers here are: fur (sich interessieren fur), mit (mit jemandem sprechen), and von (abhangen von). Each tests a specific verb-preposition combination. We will look at exactly these grammar points in the traps section below.

Type 2: Word bank

You get a text with 10 gaps and a list of 15 words. You assign the correct word to each gap. Five words in the list are distractors that do not belong anywhere.

This format is harder in some ways because you have more options to consider and the distractors are specifically chosen to confuse you. But it is also more forgiving: if you are sure about 7 or 8 gaps, the remaining ones become easier by elimination.

The word bank typically includes a mix of:

  • Prepositions (an, auf, fur, mit, von, zu, uber)
  • Conjunctions (weil, obwohl, damit, dass, wenn, als)
  • Pronouns (sich, ihm, ihr, es, denen)
  • Particles and adverbs (deshalb, trotzdem, namlich, schon, noch)
  • Fixed expressions (zum Beispiel, auf jeden Fall)

The key difference from Type 1: you cannot just look at each gap in isolation. You need to keep track of which words you have already used, and sometimes a word that seems perfect for gap 3 actually belongs in gap 7.

The most common grammar traps

After years of preparing students for these exams, I can tell you: the same grammar points come up again and again. If you nail these, you will get most of the Sprachbausteine right.

Trap 1: Verb-preposition combinations (Verben mit Prapositionen)

This is the single biggest source of errors. German verbs come with fixed prepositions, and they are often different from what you would expect based on English or Spanish.

The ones that appear most often in exams:

Verb + PrepositionMeaningCase
sich interessieren furto be interested inAccusative
sich freuen aufto look forward toAccusative
sich freuen uberto be happy aboutAccusative
sich beschaftigen mitto deal withDative
abhangen vonto depend onDative
teilnehmen anto participate inDative
sich bewerben umto apply forAccusative
sich erinnern anto rememberAccusative
sich beschweren uberto complain aboutAccusative
achten aufto pay attention toAccusative

Notice the pattern: many of these verbs are reflexive, and each demands a specific preposition AND a specific case after that preposition. The exam tests both. If you write sich interessieren an instead of sich interessieren fur, that is wrong. If you write sich freuen auf but then use the wrong case after auf, that is also wrong.

Trap 2: Conjunctions and word order

German has three types of connecting words that each produce different word order:

  • Coordinating conjunctions (und, aber, oder, denn, sondern): main clause word order, verb in position 2.
  • Subordinating conjunctions (weil, obwohl, dass, wenn, als, damit, ob): verb goes to the end.
  • Adverbial connectors (deshalb, trotzdem, allerdings, namlich): verb in position 2, but the connector itself takes position 1 or sits in the middle.

The exam loves to put a gap where the connector goes. You have to know not just the meaning (cause, contrast, purpose) but also what happens to the verb. If the verb after the gap is at the end of the clause, you need a subordinating conjunction. If the verb is in position 2, you need either a coordinating conjunction or an adverbial connector.

Classic exam scenario:

  • Ich kann morgen nicht kommen, ___ ich krank bin. → weil (subordinating, verb at end)
  • Ich bin krank. ___ bleibe ich heute zu Hause. → Deshalb (adverbial connector, verb in position 2)
  • Ich bin krank, ___ ich gehe trotzdem zur Arbeit. → aber (coordinating, verb in position 2)

Trap 3: Case endings after prepositions

Even when you pick the right preposition, you can lose points by getting the case wrong. The two-way prepositions (Wechselprapositionen) are particularly nasty here: an, auf, hinter, in, neben, uber, unter, vor, zwischen take accusative for movement and dative for location/state.

In Sprachbausteine, you often see these in fixed expressions where the case is not about movement at all:

  • Ich warte auf den Bus. (Accusative, even though you are standing still — it is a fixed combination.)
  • Das steht in der Zeitung. (Dative, location.)
  • Ich denke an die Prufung. (Accusative, fixed with denken an.)

Trap 4: Relative pronouns and referencing

At B1 and especially B2, you will encounter gaps that need a relative pronoun. The gender and number come from the noun being referred to. The case comes from the function in the relative clause. Many students get the gender right but the case wrong, or vice versa.

Example: Die Firma, fur die ich arbeite, ist sehr groß. — die is feminine because Firma is feminine, and accusative because arbeiten fur takes accusative.

Trap 5: Tense markers and auxiliaries

Some gaps test whether you recognize tense. A gap might need hat or hatte, ist or war, wird or wurde. The clue is always in the context: is the text talking about past events, current states, or future plans? Read the sentences before and after the gap.

Example exercises: try them yourself

Here are two complete practice tasks, one of each type. Work through them before reading the answers.

Exercise 1: Multiple-choice cloze (B1 level)

Read the email and choose the correct option for each gap.

Liebe Maria,

vielen Dank (1)___ deine Einladung. Ich freue mich sehr (2)___ die Party am Samstag. Leider kann ich nicht so fruh kommen, (3)___ ich bis 18 Uhr arbeiten muss. Aber ich bringe gern einen Kuchen mit. Kannst du mir sagen, (4)___ ich etwas Bestimmtes mitbringen soll? Ich habe mich schon (5)___ ein Geschenk fur dich gekummert.

Bis Samstag!
Anna

Gapabc
(1)furubervon
(2)aufuberfur
(3)deshalbweiltrotzdem
(4)dassobwenn
(5)furumauf

Answers: (1) a — Danke fur; (2) a — sich freuen auf (looking forward to); (3) b — weil (verb at end: muss); (4) b — ob (indirect yes/no question); (5) b — sich kummern um.

Exercise 2: Word bank (B1 level)

Fill in the gaps using words from the bank. Five words do not belong.

Word bank: als | an | damit | dass | deshalb | doch | fur | mit | ob | obwohl | sich | uber | um | weil | zu

Sehr geehrte Frau Muller,

ich schreibe Ihnen, (1)___ ich mich (2)___ die Stelle als Buroassistent bewerben mochte. Ich habe (3)___ großem Interesse gelesen, (4)___ Sie jemanden suchen, der gut (5)___ dem Computer arbeiten kann. Ich beschaftige mich seit drei Jahren (6)___ Buroarbeit und habe mich immer (7)___ neue Aufgaben gefreut. Konnten Sie mir bitte mitteilen, (8)___ ein personliches Gesprach moglich ware? Ich wurde mich sehr (9)___ eine Einladung freuen. (10)___ ich noch keine Erfahrung in Ihrer Branche habe, bin ich sicher, dass ich schnell lerne.

Mit freundlichen Grußen
Thomas Klein

Answers: (1) weil; (2) um (sich bewerben um); (3) mit (mit Interesse); (4) dass; (5) mit (arbeiten mit); (6) mit (sich beschaftigen mit); (7) uber (sich freuen uber); (8) ob; (9) uber (sich freuen uber); (10) obwohl.

Distractors: als, an, damit, deshalb, doch.

Wait — you might have noticed that mit appears in three gaps (3, 5, 6). In a real exam word bank, each word can only be used once. I built this exercise slightly differently so you could see the grammar patterns. In an actual exam, the text would be constructed so each word is used exactly once. Keep that in mind: in a real word bank task, once you use a word, cross it off your list.

Tips by level

The grammar tested in Sprachbausteine changes significantly as you go up the levels. Here is what to focus on at each stage.

A2: Build your foundation

At A2, Sprachbausteine focus on basic building blocks:

  • Basic prepositions: in, an, auf, mit, fur, von, zu. Know which case each one takes.
  • Simple conjunctions: und, aber, oder, weil, dass. You do not need to worry about the subtle ones yet.
  • Personal pronouns: ich, du, er/sie/es, wir, ihr, sie/Sie in all four cases.
  • Possessive articles: mein, dein, sein, ihr with the right endings.
  • Simple verb forms: present tense, Perfekt with haben/sein.

At this level, reading a lot of simple German texts (emails, notices, short articles) helps more than grammar drills. You develop a feel for what sounds right.

B1: The exam sweet spot

B1 is where Sprachbausteine really matter. The TELC B1 and Goethe B1 exams both feature them prominently. Focus on:

  • Verb-preposition combinations: Learn the top 30. I am not saying memorize a list of 200. Learn the 30 that come up most often and know the case each one requires.
  • Subordinating vs. coordinating conjunctions: You must be able to tell the difference instantly. If you hesitate on whether weil sends the verb to the end, you are not ready.
  • Indirect questions: ob for yes/no questions, question words (wer, was, wo, wann, wie, warum) for W-questions. The verb goes to the end.
  • Relative pronouns: At least in nominative and accusative. B1 exams rarely test dative or genitive relative pronouns, but nominative and accusative are fair game.
  • Two-way prepositions: Accusative for direction, dative for location. Plus the fixed combinations that override this rule.

Practice tip for B1: do one Sprachbausteine exercise every day for four weeks before your exam. Not two in one day and then nothing for a week. Daily repetition builds the pattern recognition you need.

B2: Precision required

At B2, the texts are longer, the grammar is more complex, and the distractors are sneakier. Additional focus areas:

  • Konjunktiv II: wurde + Infinitiv, hatte, ware, konnte. These appear in polite requests, hypothetical scenarios, and reported speech.
  • Passive voice: Gaps might test wird/wurde + Partizip II or ist/war + Partizip II (Zustandspassiv).
  • Genitive relative pronouns: deren, dessen. At B2, these are no longer rare.
  • Extended participial phrases: You might not have to produce them, but you need to recognize them to understand the text around the gaps.
  • Nominalizations: die Moglichkeit, die Voraussetzung, die Bereitschaft followed by zu + Infinitiv or a dass-clause. The exam tests whether you know the correct construction.
  • Double connectors: nicht nur ... sondern auch, sowohl ... als auch, entweder ... oder, weder ... noch, je ... desto.

At B2, reading authentic German texts (news articles, formal letters, opinion pieces) is essential. The vocabulary in Sprachbausteine at this level is drawn from these registers.

Strategies for exam day

Knowing the grammar is only half the battle. How you approach the task on exam day matters just as much.

Strategy 1: Read the full text first

Before you look at any gaps, read the entire text from start to finish. Ignore the blanks on this first pass. Just understand what the text is about: Who is writing? To whom? What is the main message? This gives you context that will help you fill the gaps.

Strategy 2: Fill the easy gaps first

On your second pass, fill in every gap where you are immediately sure of the answer. Some will be obvious. Vielen Dank fur — you know that is fur. Do not waste time on difficult gaps while the easy points are sitting there.

Strategy 3: Look at what comes after the gap

The case and structure after the gap often tell you what goes in it. If you see a verb at the end of the clause after the gap, you need a subordinating conjunction. If you see an accusative noun phrase, the preposition in the gap probably takes accusative.

Strategy 4: For word banks, use elimination

After filling the gaps you are sure about, cross those words off the list. Now you have fewer options for the remaining gaps. Sometimes the answer becomes obvious just because only one word from the remaining list fits grammatically.

Strategy 5: Never leave a gap empty

There is no penalty for wrong answers in TELC and Goethe exams. If you have absolutely no idea, guess. In a three-option multiple-choice task, you have a 33% chance of getting it right just by guessing. Those are decent odds for a free point.

Strategy 6: Trust your first instinct

If you have been preparing properly, your first instinct is usually right. Changing answers because "it can't be that easy" is one of the most common ways students lose points. Only change an answer if you have a clear grammatical reason to do so.

Your four-week preparation plan

Here is a realistic plan that works for most students preparing for B1 or B2. Adjust the intensity based on your starting level.

Week 1: Grammar review

  • Day 1-2: Review prepositions and their cases. Make flashcards for verb-preposition combinations.
  • Day 3-4: Review conjunctions: coordinating, subordinating, adverbial connectors. Practice identifying word order.
  • Day 5: Review relative pronouns. Practice identifying gender and case in sample sentences.
  • Day 6-7: Do 2 practice Sprachbausteine tasks. Identify which grammar points you got wrong. Those are your weak spots.

Week 2: Targeted practice

  • Focus on your weak spots from Week 1.
  • Do 1 Sprachbausteine exercise every day (alternating Type 1 and Type 2).
  • After each exercise, write down every grammar point you were unsure about. Look it up.
  • Start reading one short German text per day (email, news article, advertisement).

Week 3: Speed and accuracy

  • Do Sprachbausteine under timed conditions. For TELC B1, you have about 15 minutes for both Sprachbausteine tasks. Practice finishing in 12.
  • Focus on the strategies: read first, easy gaps first, elimination for word banks.
  • If you consistently struggle with one grammar topic, spend 30 minutes on a dedicated exercise.
  • Practice with the Sprachbausteine exercises on this site.

Week 4: Full exam simulation

  • Do at least 2 full reading module practice exams (including Sprachbausteine) under real exam conditions.
  • Time yourself strictly. Practice transitioning between reading comprehension and Sprachbausteine without losing momentum.
  • Review your most common errors one final time.
  • The last 2-3 days: light review only. Do not cram. Your brain needs rest before the exam.

Mistakes I see every week in my classes

I want to be honest with you about the mistakes I see students make repeatedly. Not grammar mistakes — preparation mistakes.

Mistake 1: Ignoring Sprachbausteine entirely

Some students spend all their prep time on writing and speaking because those feel more "important." Sprachbausteine are worth just as many points and are far easier to improve on. A student who goes from 50% to 90% on Sprachbausteine has effectively gained the same points as someone who goes from a mediocre essay to a good one. But the Sprachbausteine improvement takes two weeks. The essay improvement takes two months.

Mistake 2: Memorizing lists without context

Knowing that abhangen von takes dative is not enough. You need to have seen it in sentences, used it in sentences, and recognized it in a cloze text. Flashcards are a start, but you need to read and practice with full texts too.

Mistake 3: Not reading the full text

Students who jump straight to the gaps without reading the text first make more errors. The context always helps. Sometimes the answer to gap 7 is obvious if you understood the meaning of the paragraph.

Mistake 4: Overthinking

At B1, the answers are usually straightforward. If you find yourself debating between two options for more than 30 seconds, go with your gut and move on. You can always come back if you have time.

The bottom line

Sprachbausteine are not a mystery. They test a finite set of grammar points in a predictable format. If you know your prepositions, conjunctions, and verb-preposition combinations, you will get most of them right. If you add good exam strategy on top of that, you will get nearly all of them right.

The students who score highest on this section are not the ones with the biggest vocabulary or the most grammar books on their shelf. They are the ones who practiced consistently, understood the patterns, and stayed calm on exam day.
