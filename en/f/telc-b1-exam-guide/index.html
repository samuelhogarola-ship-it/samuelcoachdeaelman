TELC B1 Exam Preparation

TELC B1 Exam: Structure, Timing & Preparation Tips

If you are preparing for the TELC Deutsch B1 exam and the whole thing feels like a black box, you are in the right place. This guide walks you through every single section, tells you exactly how much time you get, how scoring works, and gives you seven concrete tips to prepare without wasting weeks on the wrong stuff.

Published 5 September 2026 12 min read Level B1

What you will find here

  • A clear overview of both exam parts and the 300-point total.
  • Each section broken down: Reading, Sprachbausteine, Listening, Writing, Speaking.
  • The scoring table so you know exactly what you need to pass.
  • A realistic exam day schedule from start to finish.
  • Seven preparation tips that actually make a difference.

TELC B1 at a glance: two parts, 300 points

The TELC Deutsch B1 exam is split into two separate parts. The written exam (Schriftliche Pruefung) covers reading, grammar, listening and writing. The oral exam (Muendliche Pruefung) is the speaking test, usually done in pairs on the same day or the day after the written part.

The maximum total score is 300 points: 150 for the written part and 150 for the oral part. You need at least 60% in each part to pass, which means a minimum of 90 points written and 90 points spoken. You cannot compensate a weak speaking result with a strong written one or the other way around.

Both parts are independent. Failing one means you fail the whole exam, even if you scored perfectly on the other. So do not neglect speaking just because you feel safer with grammar exercises.

The written exam lasts about 2 hours and 30 minutes. The oral exam takes around 15 minutes per pair. Let us go through each section in detail.

Reading comprehension (Leseverstehen) -- 3 parts, 75 points

Reading is the first thing you face on exam day. You get 65 minutes for the entire reading section, and it is divided into three distinct parts. There is no separate time for each part -- you manage your own time across all three.

Part 1: Global understanding (5 items, 25 points)

You receive a set of short texts -- usually newspaper headlines, brief announcements or classified ads -- and you have to match them to descriptions or situations. The goal here is not to understand every word. They want to see whether you can grasp the general meaning and match it to the right context.

This part rewards speed. Read the descriptions first, then scan the texts for keywords. Do not get stuck trying to understand every detail. If a text mentions "Wohnung" and "ab sofort", you probably have a rental listing. Think in categories, not sentences.

Part 2: Detailed understanding (5 items, 25 points)

Here you get a longer text -- an article, a letter or a report -- and five multiple-choice questions about it. This is where they test whether you can understand specific information, not just the gist. You need to find exact details, sometimes hidden in subordinate clauses or rephrased in tricky ways.

The classic trap: two answer options look almost identical, but one has a small twist. Read the question carefully, go back to the text, find the exact passage. Do not rely on memory. If you want to practice this type of reading at B1 level, take a look at the B1 reading comprehension exercises on this site.

Part 3: Selective understanding (10 items, 25 points)

You see a set of short texts -- often event listings, programme schedules or product descriptions -- and ten statements. You have to decide which text matches which statement, or whether the information is mentioned at all. Some items have no match.

This part tests scanning skills. Do not read every text from top to bottom. Read the statement, identify the key information, then look for it across the texts. Speed matters here because ten items is a lot for the time you have left.

Time management in the reading section is everything. Many candidates spend too long on Part 2 and then rush through Part 3. Practice with a timer from day one.

Sprachbausteine -- 2 parts, 30 points

Sprachbausteine is the section that tests your grammar and vocabulary in context. You get 15 minutes for both parts combined. It comes right after the reading section, still within the written exam.

Part 1: Grammar in context (10 items, 15 points)

You read a text with ten gaps. For each gap, you choose the correct word from three options. These are grammar-heavy: prepositions, conjunctions, verb forms, articles. The text is usually a semi-formal letter or a short article.

What trips people up: the three options often look very similar. You might see "als", "wenn" and "wann" for the same gap. If you do not know the rule, you are guessing. The good news is that the grammar tested here follows predictable patterns. Practising with Sprachbausteine exercises makes a real difference because the same structures come up again and again.

Part 2: Vocabulary in context (10 items, 15 points)

Same format as Part 1, but the focus shifts to vocabulary and collocations. The gaps test whether you know which word fits naturally in a given context. This is less about grammar rules and more about "does this sound right."

At B1 level, the vocabulary tested is fairly standard: everyday situations, work, health, travel, social life. The challenge is that some options are correct German words that simply do not fit that particular sentence. Reading regularly in German -- news, short articles, even social media posts -- builds the kind of intuition you need here.

Listening comprehension (Hoerverstehen) -- 3 parts, 75 points

The listening section lasts about 30 minutes. You hear each recording only once in Parts 1 and 3, and twice in Part 2. The audio is played for the entire room at the same time -- there are no headphones and no rewind.

Part 1: Global understanding (5 items, 25 points)

Five short recordings -- typically announcements, voicemail messages or brief conversations. For each one, you answer a multiple-choice question or match a statement. You hear each recording once.

The key here is to read the questions before the audio plays. You usually get a few seconds between recordings. Use that time to read ahead. If you already know what the question asks, you can listen with a purpose instead of trying to catch everything.

Part 2: Detailed understanding (10 items, 25 points)

One longer recording -- a radio interview, a conversation or a discussion -- with ten true/false statements. You hear this one twice, which is a relief because the text is more complex and the statements are more nuanced.

On the first listen, mark the answers you are sure about and flag the ones you are not. On the second listen, focus only on the flagged items. Do not change answers you were confident about unless you clearly hear something different. Your first instinct is usually right.

Part 3: Selective understanding (5 items, 25 points)

Five short recordings again, similar to Part 1 but with a different focus. You listen for specific information -- a price, a date, a name, a location. You hear each one once.

This part rewards focus. The answer is usually one specific word or number buried in a 30-second clip. If you miss it, it is gone. Train yourself to listen for data points, not for understanding the whole situation. When you practise, write down numbers, dates and names as you hear them -- that habit will save you on exam day.

The listening section is where most candidates lose points they did not expect to lose. Practice with real TELC mock exams, not just textbook audio. The speed and background noise are different.

Writing (Schriftlicher Ausdruck) -- 45 points

The writing task gives you 30 minutes to write one text. You receive a prompt -- typically a situation that requires a semi-formal or informal letter or email. For example: you need to cancel a booking, complain about a product, respond to an invitation, or ask for information.

You are evaluated on four criteria:

  • Content: Did you address all the points in the prompt?
  • Communicative design: Is the text structured logically? Does it have an appropriate greeting and closing?
  • Correctness: Grammar, spelling and punctuation.
  • Vocabulary range: Do you use varied language or repeat the same three phrases?

The most common mistake is ignoring one of the prompt points. The task usually gives you three or four things to address. If you skip one, you lose points on content no matter how perfectly you write the rest. Read the prompt twice, underline each point, and tick them off as you write.

Keep your sentences clear. B1 is not the level where you need to impress with nested subordinate clauses. A well-structured text with simple but correct sentences scores higher than an ambitious text full of errors.

Speaking (Muendliche Pruefung) -- 3 parts, 150 points

The oral exam is done in pairs with two examiners. One examiner leads the conversation, the other takes notes. The whole thing lasts about 15 minutes per pair. You get 20 minutes of preparation time before the exam starts -- use every single minute of it.

Part 1: Contact conversation (about 3 minutes)

Both candidates introduce themselves and have a brief, natural conversation. The examiner might ask follow-up questions: where you live, what you do, why you are learning German, what your hobbies are.

This part is a warm-up, but do not underestimate it. Examiners form a first impression here. Speak clearly, make eye contact, and avoid giving one-word answers. If they ask "Warum lernen Sie Deutsch?", give a real answer with at least two or three sentences, not just "Fuer die Arbeit."

Part 2: Conversation about a topic (about 6 minutes)

Each candidate gets a prompt card with a topic and guiding questions. You speak about your topic for about a minute, then discuss both topics with your partner. Typical themes: healthy eating, social media, learning a language, travel, city vs. countryside.

Structure matters here. Start with a brief statement, give one or two reasons, mention a personal example if you can, and close with a short conclusion. When your partner speaks, react to what they say. Agreement, disagreement, follow-up questions -- the examiners want to see interaction, not two parallel monologues.

Part 3: Joint task (about 6 minutes)

You and your partner plan something together: a trip, a party, a gift for a colleague, a class outing. You get a prompt card with the situation and some points to discuss. The goal is to reach a decision together.

This is the part where many candidates struggle because it requires genuine interaction. You need to suggest ideas, respond to your partner's suggestions, negotiate and compromise. Do not just list your ideas and then say "ja, gut." Ask your partner questions: "Was meinst du?", "Hast du eine andere Idee?", "Sollen wir lieber...?" The examiners score your ability to cooperate, not just your grammar.

The speaking exam is worth 150 points -- exactly half the total. Candidates who only prepare reading and grammar are gambling with 50% of their grade.

Scoring breakdown: how the 300 points are distributed

Here is the full scoring table so you know exactly what each section is worth and what you need to pass.

Section Part Items Points
Leseverstehen (Reading) Part 1 -- Global 5 25
Part 2 -- Detailed 5 25
Part 3 -- Selective 10 25
Sprachbausteine Part 1 -- Grammar 10 15
Part 2 -- Vocabulary 10 15
Hoerverstehen (Listening) Parts 1-3 20 75
Schriftlicher Ausdruck (Writing) 1 task -- 45
Written total 150
Muendliche Pruefung (Speaking) Part 1 -- Contact -- ~25
Part 2 -- Topic -- ~50
Part 3 -- Joint task -- ~75
Oral total 150
Grand total 300

To pass you need at least 60% in each part: 90 out of 150 written and 90 out of 150 oral. The certificate shows a grade from "sehr gut" (very good) to "ausreichend" (sufficient), but most people just care about passing. If you score 180/300 total but only 80/150 on speaking, you fail -- that 60% rule per part is absolute.

Exam day schedule: what to expect hour by hour

Knowing the schedule in advance removes one layer of stress. Here is roughly how exam day unfolds at most TELC centres.

Time What happens
08:30 - 09:00 Registration, ID check, seating
09:00 - 10:05 Reading comprehension (65 min) + Sprachbausteine (15 min)
10:05 - 10:25 Break (20 min)
10:25 - 10:55 Listening comprehension (30 min)
10:55 - 11:25 Writing (30 min)
11:25 - 12:30 Break + preparation time for speaking (20 min prep)
12:30 onwards Speaking exam in pairs (~15 min per pair)

Some centres run the speaking exam on a different day. Check with your exam centre in advance so you know whether to plan for one long day or two shorter ones.

Bring two forms of ID (passport or national ID card plus a photocopy), several pens (blue or black ink, no pencil for the answer sheet), and a bottle of water. You cannot bring dictionaries, phones or any electronic devices into the exam room.

7 preparation tips that actually work

Here is what I tell every student who walks into my B1 preparation sessions. These are not generic "study hard" tips -- they come from watching hundreds of candidates succeed or fail at this specific exam.

1. Learn the exam format before you learn any content

The single biggest advantage you can give yourself is knowing exactly what each section looks like, how many items it has, and how much time you get. When you sit down on exam day, nothing should surprise you. Download a TELC B1 mock exam from the official TELC website, print it out, and work through it under real conditions. Do this before you start any focused preparation.

2. Practice reading with a timer from week one

65 minutes for three reading parts sounds generous until you are actually sitting there. Most candidates run out of time in the reading section at least once during preparation. Set a timer every single time you practice. Aim for 20 minutes on Part 1, 25 on Part 2, and 20 on Part 3. If you finish faster, great -- use the extra minutes to double-check. The B1 Leseverstehen exercises on this site are timed and structured the same way as the real exam.

3. Do not study Sprachbausteine in isolation

Sprachbausteine tests grammar and vocabulary in context. Studying grammar rules from a table is not enough. You need to see prepositions, conjunctions and verb forms inside real sentences. Use Sprachbausteine practice exercises that replicate the exam format: a text with gaps and three options per gap. Do them regularly, review your mistakes, and look for patterns. If you keep getting "als" vs. "wenn" wrong, that is the grammar point you need to drill.

4. Train your ears with real-speed audio

Textbook listening exercises are usually slower and cleaner than the TELC recordings. Get used to natural-speed German by listening to podcasts, radio news (Deutsche Welle has B1-level content), or TELC sample audio. The jump from classroom audio to exam audio catches a lot of people off guard. Start training your ears early.

5. Write at least ten practice letters before exam day

The writing task is predictable. The topics rotate around a small set of everyday situations: cancelling something, requesting information, responding to an invitation, filing a complaint, thanking someone. Write at least ten practice texts covering different scenarios. Each time, check that you addressed every point in the prompt, used an appropriate greeting and closing, and stayed under the word count (about 150-180 words is ideal at B1). Have someone correct them if possible -- errors you do not notice will repeat themselves on exam day.

6. Practice speaking with a real partner, not a mirror

Parts 2 and 3 of the speaking exam require interaction. You cannot prepare for interaction alone. Find a study partner, a language exchange partner, or book some conversation sessions with a tutor. Practice the three parts of the oral exam in order, with a timer. Get used to reacting spontaneously, asking follow-up questions, and negotiating a decision. If you need structured exam preparation with a coach who knows the TELC format inside out, that option is available too.

7. Simulate the full exam at least twice

Before exam day, do at least two full mock exams under realistic conditions: same time limits, no phone, no dictionary, no breaks outside the scheduled ones. This does two things. First, it reveals your weak spots while you still have time to work on them. Second, it builds endurance. The TELC B1 is a long exam, and mental fatigue is real. If you have already sat through the whole thing twice, your brain knows what to expect.

Preparation without simulation is like training for a marathon by only running short distances. The exam is a test of stamina as much as knowledge.

Quick note: TELC B1 vs. Goethe B1

People often ask which exam is easier. The honest answer: they are different, not easier or harder. The TELC B1 has Sprachbausteine as a separate section, which the Goethe does not. The Goethe B1 has a different writing format and the speaking exam works a bit differently. Both are equally recognized for citizenship applications, university admission and job requirements in German-speaking countries.

If you want to compare in detail, read the Goethe B1 exam guide on this site. The choice usually comes down to which format suits your strengths and which exam centre is closer to where you live.

What to do next

You now have a clear picture of what the TELC B1 exam looks like, how it is scored, and what to focus on during preparation. Here is a concrete next step for each section:

  • Reading: Start with the B1 reading exercises and practice with a 65-minute timer.
  • Sprachbausteine: Work through the Sprachbausteine practice sets and track which grammar patterns you get wrong.
  • Listening: Download a TELC mock exam and listen to the audio at least three times. First time: answer the questions. Second time: check your answers. Third time: read the transcript while listening.
  • Writing: Pick a prompt and write a letter in 30 minutes, right now. Then check it against the four evaluation criteria listed above.
  • Speaking: Find a partner and run through all three parts with a timer. If you do not have a partner yet, get in touch and we can work on this together.

For a broader look at how Leseverstehen exercises can sharpen your reading across all levels, check out Leseverstehen practice: tips and strategies for A1-C1. And if you want a structured approach to your entire B1 preparation, the exam preparation page has everything in one place.

The TELC B1 is not a test you can wing. But it is also not a test you need to fear. If you know the format, practice the right things, and simulate the exam a couple of times, you will walk in there knowing exactly what to expect. And that is already half the battle.
