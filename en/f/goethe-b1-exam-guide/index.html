Goethe-Zertifikat B1: Structure, Scoring & How to Prepare

The Goethe-Zertifikat B1 is one of the most recognized German language certificates worldwide. Whether you need it for a visa, university admission, or professional reasons, this guide breaks down every module, explains the scoring system, compares it to the TELC B1, and gives you a clear 6-step preparation plan.

September 5, 2026 12 min read Level B1

What you will learn here

  • How the Goethe B1 is structured: 4 independent modules.
  • Exactly what happens in each module (Reading, Listening, Writing, Speaking).
  • The scoring system: 100 points per module, 60 to pass.
  • How Goethe B1 compares to TELC B1.
  • A concrete 6-step preparation plan you can follow today.

Why the Goethe-Zertifikat B1 matters

The Goethe-Zertifikat B1 proves you can handle everyday German independently. At B1, you understand the main points of clear standard language on familiar topics like work, school, and leisure. You can deal with most situations you encounter while traveling in German-speaking countries. You can produce simple connected text on familiar topics and describe experiences, events, hopes, and ambitions.

That is the official CEFR definition. In practical terms, the B1 certificate is the most commonly required level for:

  • German citizenship applications (Einbürgerung) -- B1 is the minimum.
  • Spouse visa requirements (though A1 is the legal minimum, many consulates expect B1 for smoother processing).
  • Ausbildung (vocational training) -- most programs require B1 or B2.
  • University prep programs (Studienkolleg) -- B1 is typically the entry requirement.
  • Professional licensing -- nurses, doctors, and other regulated professions need at least B1 as a starting point.

The Goethe-Institut is one of two major providers of officially recognized B1 exams in Germany, the other being TELC. Both certificates carry the same legal weight. The Goethe exam, however, is offered at Goethe-Institut centers worldwide, which makes it the go-to choice for learners outside Germany.

Overall structure: 4 independent modules

This is the single most important thing to understand about the Goethe B1: it consists of four completely independent modules. You can take them all on the same day, or you can spread them out over different exam dates. If you fail one module, you only need to retake that module, not the entire exam.

The four modules are:

ModuleDurationMax. pointsPass mark
Lesen (Reading)65 minutes10060
Hören (Listening)approx. 40 minutes10060
Schreiben (Writing)60 minutes10060
Sprechen (Speaking)approx. 15 minutes10060

Each module is worth exactly 100 points. You need at least 60 points in each module to pass it. There is no "overall" average -- every module stands on its own. This is good news if you are strong in some areas and weaker in others: you can focus your retake energy on the module that gave you trouble.

Key fact: You have up to one year from your first module attempt to complete all four modules. After that, any passed modules expire and you start over. Plan accordingly.

Module 1: Lesen (Reading) -- 65 minutes

The Reading module tests your ability to understand written German across different text types. You get 65 minutes to work through five parts. That sounds generous, but most candidates find it tight because Part 4 and Part 5 require careful reading.

Part 1: Blog comments or forum posts

You read a blog post or article and then six short reader comments about it. For each comment, you decide whether the person agrees with the author, partially agrees, or disagrees. This is about understanding opinions and positions, not factual details.

Points: 6 items, each worth a set number of points toward the module total.

Part 2: Newspaper or magazine article

A longer text (around 400-450 words) with six multiple-choice questions. Each question has three options (a, b, c). The questions follow the order of the text, so you can work through it paragraph by paragraph. The challenge here is distinguishing between what the text actually says and what seems plausible.

Part 3: Classified ads or short notices

You get seven situations (for example: "Maria wants to find a used bicycle for her son") and ten short ads or notices. You match each situation to the correct ad, or mark that no ad fits. Two situations will have no matching ad -- those are the tricky ones.

Part 4: Reader letters or opinion pieces

Seven statements about a text. You decide whether each statement is true (Richtig) or false (Falsch). The text is usually an opinion letter or editorial, and the statements test whether you can separate the author's opinion from facts mentioned in the text.

Part 5: Instructions or regulations

A formal text like house rules (Hausordnung), terms and conditions, or workplace regulations. Four multiple-choice questions test whether you understand the rules described. This part often uses formal, legal-sounding language that many learners find challenging.

Reading strategy: Do not read the full text first. Read the questions, underline key words, then scan the text for those words and their synonyms. This saves you at least 10 minutes across the module. Practice this approach with our B1 reading comprehension exercises.

Module 2: Hören (Listening) -- approx. 40 minutes

The Listening module plays audio recordings and asks you to answer questions about what you heard. You hear each recording either once or twice, depending on the part. The total time includes the audio playback and short pauses for answering.

Part 1: Everyday conversations

You hear ten short dialogues or monologues (announcements, phone messages, conversations). For each one, you answer a true/false question. You hear each recording once only. This part moves fast -- the key is reading the statement before each audio starts.

Part 2: Radio interview or talk

A longer conversation or interview (about 3-4 minutes). Five multiple-choice questions with three options each. You hear the recording once. The questions follow the order of the conversation, so track them as you listen.

Part 3: Group discussion

A conversation among several people discussing a topic. Seven true/false questions about who said what or what opinions were expressed. You hear this recording once. The challenge is distinguishing between the speakers and tracking their individual positions.

Part 4: Radio feature or presentation

A monologue-style recording -- someone giving a talk, a radio feature, or a guided tour. Seven multiple-choice questions. You hear this recording twice. This is the only part where you get a second chance, so use the first listen to get the general idea and the second to confirm your answers.

Listening tip: The biggest mistake candidates make is panicking when they miss something. If you did not catch the answer, mark your best guess and move on. The audio will not wait. Dwelling on one missed question means missing the next two.

Module 3: Schreiben (Writing) -- 60 minutes

The Writing module has three tasks. You write real-world texts: an email, a forum post, and a formal message. The examiners evaluate grammar, vocabulary, task completion, and coherence -- but they are looking for B1-level German, not perfection. A text with a few grammatical errors but clear communication will score well.

Task 1: Personal email (approx. 80 words)

You write an email to a friend, colleague, or acquaintance about a specific situation. You receive three or four content points you must address (for example: react to news, make a suggestion, explain a reason, ask a question). You need to hit all the content points to get full marks on task completion.

Time suggestion: 20 minutes.

Task 2: Forum or blog post (approx. 80 words)

You write a post or comment for an online discussion forum. The topic is given, and you need to express and justify your opinion, respond to another person's view, and make a suggestion or proposal. The register here is semi-formal -- not as casual as the email, but not stiff either.

Time suggestion: 25 minutes.

Task 3: Semi-formal or formal email (approx. 40 words)

A short formal email -- for instance, requesting information from a language school, canceling an appointment, or complaining about a product. You get one or two content points. The focus is on formal register: correct salutation (Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren), appropriate closing (Mit freundlichen Grüßen), and polite formulations (Ich möchte Sie bitten..., Könnten Sie mir bitte...).

Time suggestion: 15 minutes.

Writing strategy: Start with Task 3. It is the shortest and most formulaic -- you can finish it in 10 minutes and free up time for the longer tasks. Then do Task 1, then Task 2. Always leave 5 minutes at the end to re-read everything for obvious errors (missing verbs, wrong word order, forgotten umlauts).

Module 4: Sprechen (Speaking) -- approx. 15 minutes

The Speaking module is done in pairs (sometimes groups of three if there is an odd number). Two examiners are present: one leads the exam, the other takes notes and scores. You get a short preparation time before the exam starts.

Part 1: Presentation (3-4 minutes)

You prepare a short presentation on a given topic (for example: "Living in the city vs. the countryside" or "Should children have smartphones?"). You receive a card with the topic and four guiding points: introduce the topic, describe the situation in your home country, give advantages and disadvantages, and state your opinion. After your presentation, your partner asks you a question, and you answer it.

Part 2: Discussion with your partner (3-4 minutes)

You and your partner discuss a practical situation together. For example: "You and your friend want to organize a birthday party. Discuss and agree on the details: location, food, guests, activities." The examiners want to see that you can make suggestions, react to your partner's ideas, negotiate, and reach a compromise.

Part 3: Problem-solving (2-3 minutes)

You see a photo or a short scenario and need to respond to it. For example: a photo of a messy shared kitchen, or a situation where a neighbor plays loud music at night. You describe what you see, react to the situation, and suggest a solution. This part tests spontaneous speech -- you get no preparation time.

Speaking tip: The examiners are not looking for a perfect accent or advanced vocabulary. They want to see that you can communicate your point, respond to your partner, and keep the conversation going. If you get stuck on a word, describe it. Paraphrasing shows communicative competence, which is exactly what B1 measures.

Scoring: how points translate to grades

Each module uses a 0-100 point scale. The passing threshold is always 60 points. Here is how the grades work:

PointsGradeResult
100 - 90Sehr gutPass -- very good
89 - 80GutPass -- good
79 - 70BefriedigendPass -- satisfactory
69 - 60AusreichendPass -- adequate
59 - 0Nicht bestandenFail

Your certificate shows the grade for each module individually. There is no combined grade. If someone asks "What grade did you get?", the answer is four separate grades, one per module. For most practical purposes (visa, job applications), all that matters is that every module shows Bestanden (passed).

How points are distributed within each module

Reading and Listening: These are objectively scored. Each correct answer is worth a fixed number of points. There is no partial credit -- your answer is either right or wrong. The exact point distribution varies slightly between exam versions, but the total always adds up to 100.

Writing: Two trained examiners score your texts independently. They evaluate four criteria: task completion (did you address all content points?), coherence (does your text flow logically?), vocabulary (is it appropriate for B1?), and grammar (are structures mostly correct?). If the two examiners' scores differ significantly, a third examiner decides.

Speaking: The two examiners in the room score you on four criteria: task completion, fluency and interaction (can you keep a conversation going?), vocabulary, and grammar. Your partner's performance does not affect your score -- even if your partner struggles, you are scored on your own production.

Goethe B1 vs. TELC B1: which one should you take?

Both certificates are officially recognized for the same purposes in Germany. If you hold a Goethe B1 certificate, no institution will say "We wanted a TELC certificate instead." They are legally equivalent. But the exams themselves have some practical differences worth knowing.

FeatureGoethe B1TELC B1
Modules4 independent (Lesen, Hören, Schreiben, Sprechen)2 parts: written (Lesen + Sprachbausteine + Hören + Schreiben) and oral (Sprechen)
Retake flexibilityRetake any single moduleRetake written or oral part as a whole
SprachbausteineNot includedIncluded (grammar/vocabulary fill-in-the-blank)
Reading time65 minutesShorter reading section (combined with Sprachbausteine in 90 min)
AvailabilityGoethe-Institut centers worldwidePrimarily in Germany and some European countries
Price rangeUsually higher (varies by country)Usually lower (varies by center)
Results timeline4-6 weeks typically4-8 weeks typically
Certificate validityUnlimited (no expiration)Unlimited (no expiration)

The most significant practical difference is the modular structure. With Goethe, if you pass three modules but fail one, you retake only that one module. With TELC, if you pass the oral part but fail the written part, you retake the entire written exam (Reading + Sprachbausteine + Listening + Writing together). This makes the Goethe exam more flexible and less stressful for retakes.

The second big difference is Sprachbausteine. TELC includes a grammar and vocabulary section that Goethe does not have. If grammar gap-filling exercises are your weak point, the Goethe exam avoids them entirely. If they are your strength, you might prefer TELC because they are "free" points. You can practice Sprachbausteine with our dedicated exercises either way -- the grammar knowledge transfers.

For a deeper look at the TELC B1, read our complete TELC B1 exam guide.

My recommendation: If you are taking the exam outside Germany, Goethe is your practical choice because TELC exam centers are hard to find abroad. If you are in Germany and have access to both, choose based on your strengths: strong grammar favors TELC (Sprachbausteine give you extra scoring opportunities); weaker grammar favors Goethe (no Sprachbausteine to worry about).

Common mistakes candidates make

After coaching dozens of B1 candidates, these are the patterns I see repeatedly. Avoiding them puts you ahead of most test-takers.

Spending too much time on Part 1 of Reading.

Part 1 is about understanding opinions. Candidates over-think it and burn 20 minutes on 6 items. Aim for 10 minutes. The points-per-minute ratio is better in Parts 2 and 5.

Writing too much in Task 3 (Writing).

Task 3 asks for about 40 words. Some candidates write 120. Extra length does not earn extra points and eats into time for the longer tasks. Write the minimum that covers the content points with correct formal register.

Memorizing a presentation script (Speaking).

Examiners notice immediately when someone is reciting from memory. Your intonation changes, you lose eye contact, and if you forget a line, you freeze. Use bullet points instead. Four words on your card are better than forty memorized sentences.

Ignoring the second listen in Listening Part 4.

Some candidates stop paying attention during the second playback because they feel confident. Use the second listen to verify -- many candidates change at least one answer on the second pass, and they are usually right to do so.

Not practicing with the official format.

The Goethe-Institut publishes free sample exams (Modellsatz) on their website. Many candidates never look at them and are surprised by the format on exam day. There is no reason for this -- the practice exams are free and closely match the real thing.

6-step preparation plan

This plan assumes you already have solid A2 German and can dedicate 2-3 months to exam preparation. Adjust the timeline based on your starting level and available study time.

Step 1: Take a diagnostic test (Week 1)

Download the official Goethe B1 Modellsatz from the Goethe-Institut website. Take it under real exam conditions: time yourself, do not use a dictionary, and do not pause the listening audio. Score yourself honestly. This tells you exactly which modules need the most work.

If you score below 40 on any module, you likely need more than 3 months. If you score 50-59, you are close but need targeted practice. If you score 60+, that module just needs maintenance.

Step 2: Build your vocabulary foundation (Weeks 1-4)

The Goethe B1 expects you to handle topics like work, health, education, media, travel, housing, and current events. Build vocabulary around these themes. Do not just memorize word lists -- learn words in context. Read short German texts on these topics and note the words you do not know.

Aim for about 20 new words per day, reviewed using spaced repetition. At this pace, you add roughly 600 words in a month, which is enough to fill most B1 vocabulary gaps if your A2 base is solid.

Step 3: Target your weak module (Weeks 2-6)

Based on your diagnostic, dedicate 50% of your study time to your weakest module. Here is what targeted practice looks like for each:

  • Reading: Practice with our B1 Leseverstehen exercises. Focus on speed -- read the questions first, then scan for answers. Work on one text type per day.
  • Listening: Listen to Deutsche Welle's "Langsam gesprochene Nachrichten" daily. Then switch to normal-speed podcasts. The jump between slow and normal speed is exactly what B1 tests.
  • Writing: Write one email and one forum post every day. Time yourself. After writing, check your text against a model answer (the Modellsatz includes sample responses). Focus on connectors: deshalb, trotzdem, außerdem, einerseits... andererseits.
  • Speaking: Record yourself giving a 3-minute presentation on a random topic every day. Listen back. Are you speaking in full sentences? Are you using connectors? Can a listener follow your argument?

Step 4: Simulate exam conditions (Weeks 5-8)

Once a week, do a full exam simulation. All four modules, in order, with the clock running. This builds stamina and familiarity with the pacing. The real exam day is long (about 3.5 hours of active testing plus breaks), and fatigue is a real factor.

After each simulation, analyze your errors. Are you making the same mistakes? That is a pattern to fix. Are you running out of time? That is a pacing issue. Are you getting close but not quite there? Focus on the question types where you lose the most points.

Step 5: Polish your Speaking and Writing (Weeks 7-10)

These are the two modules where most candidates leave points on the table, and also the two where targeted practice gives the fastest improvement. For Writing, build a toolkit of 10-15 phrases you can use in any email (Vielen Dank für Ihre Nachricht, Ich schreibe Ihnen, weil..., Ich würde mich freuen, wenn...). For Speaking, practice the transition phrases that make you sound fluent even when your grammar is imperfect (Was ich damit sagen möchte..., Ich bin der Meinung, dass..., Ein weiterer Punkt ist...).

If you can, practice the Speaking module with a partner. The exam format involves interacting with another candidate, and practicing alone does not prepare you for the give-and-take of a real discussion.

Step 6: Final week -- consolidate, do not cram (Week 11-12)

The week before the exam is not the time to learn new grammar. Review your notes, do one final simulation, and then rest. On exam day, you want to be calm and well-slept, not exhausted from a last-minute grammar marathon. The difference between 58 and 62 points is usually not about knowledge -- it is about clarity and composure.

What to expect on exam day

Knowing the logistics removes one source of stress. Here is what a typical Goethe B1 exam day looks like:

  • Arrive 30 minutes early. You need to check in, show your ID, and find your seat. Late arrivals are not admitted once a module starts.
  • Bring a valid photo ID. The same one you registered with. No ID, no exam.
  • Bring pens (black or blue). Pencil is fine for the answer sheet, but check with your exam center -- some require pen.
  • No electronic devices. Phones, smartwatches, and tablets must be turned off and stored away. If your phone rings during the exam, you can be disqualified.
  • Water is usually allowed. Food typically is not. Have a good breakfast.
  • The written modules come first (Reading, Listening, Writing), usually with a short break between them. Speaking is often scheduled for the afternoon or even a different day.
  • Results arrive in 4-6 weeks. You receive them by mail or can check online, depending on the exam center. The certificate follows by mail.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the Goethe B1 exam cost?

The price varies by country and exam center. In Germany, expect around 250-280 EUR for all four modules. Taking individual modules costs proportionally less. Check the Goethe-Institut website for your local center's pricing.

Can I take the modules on different dates?

Yes. You can take one module per exam date if you want. This is useful if you pass three modules on your first attempt and only need to retake one. Remember the one-year window: all four modules must be completed within 12 months of your first attempt.

Does the Goethe B1 certificate expire?

No. The certificate itself has no expiration date. However, some institutions (especially immigration authorities) may ask for a recent certificate if yours is more than two years old. This is their policy, not a limitation of the certificate.

How long should I study for the Goethe B1?

Coming from a solid A2 level: 2-4 months of focused study, depending on how many hours per day you can dedicate. Coming from scratch (zero German): the CEFR estimates around 350-650 hours of instruction to reach B1. That is roughly 6-12 months of regular classes plus self-study.

Is the Goethe B1 harder than TELC B1?

Neither is objectively harder. They test different things in slightly different ways. Some candidates find the Goethe Reading module harder because it is longer and more text-heavy. Others find the TELC Sprachbausteine section difficult because it tests grammar knowledge explicitly. The best strategy is to try sample exams from both and see which format suits you better.

Can I prepare on my own, without a teacher?

For Reading and Listening, absolutely. These modules reward practice volume, and all the materials you need are freely available. For Writing and Speaking, self-study is possible but slower. A teacher (or at least a study partner) can give you feedback that you cannot give yourself. If budget is tight, focus your coaching hours on Writing and Speaking, and self-study Reading and Listening.
