Exam Prep A1-A2

German A1 & A2 Exam Prep: Complete Guide

Passing the A1 or A2 German exam is a real milestone. Whether you need it for a visa, university admission, or just to prove what you already know, this guide breaks down everything: what each exam expects, the grammar and vocabulary that actually show up, and a realistic study plan to get you through it.

A1 vs A2: What Is the Actual Difference?

Both levels fall under "basic user" in the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR), but they are not the same thing. The jump from A1 to A2 is bigger than most people expect.

At A1, you can handle the absolute basics. Introducing yourself, asking for directions, ordering food, understanding very short and simple sentences. Everything is present tense, the vocabulary is limited to survival situations, and nobody expects you to hold a conversation.

At A2, you can actually communicate. You talk about your daily routine, describe past events, express what you want or need, understand short texts about familiar topics. The grammar gets noticeably harder: you need past tense, modal verbs, and more complex sentence structures.

AreaA1A2
Vocabulary~800 words~1,500-2,000 words
TensesPresent onlyPresent + Perfekt + some Modalverben
Sentence structureSimple main clausesMain clauses + basic subordinate clauses (weil, dass, wenn)
ReadingSigns, forms, very short noticesShort emails, ads, simple articles
WritingFill in forms, write 2-3 sentencesShort emails, notes, messages (~40-60 words)
SpeakingIntroduce yourself, ask/answer simple questionsDescribe routines, make plans, express preferences
ListeningUnderstand single words and very short announcementsUnderstand short dialogues and announcements on everyday topics

The key takeaway: A1 is about recognizing and producing isolated pieces of language. A2 is about connecting them into something that actually sounds like communication.

Goethe vs TELC: Which Exam Should You Take?

These are the two main exam providers for German as a foreign language. Both are internationally recognized, and for most purposes (visa applications, job requirements, university prerequisites) they are treated as equivalent. But they are not identical.

Goethe-Zertifikat

Run by the Goethe-Institut, the cultural arm of the German government. The exams are well-structured and the materials are excellent. Goethe exams tend to be slightly more "academic" in feel: the reading texts are clean, the listening recordings are clear, the tasks are predictable once you know the format.

  • Goethe A1: Start Deutsch 1 -- four parts: reading, listening, writing, speaking. Duration: about 65 minutes for the written parts.
  • Goethe A2: Fit in Deutsch 2 (for young learners) or Goethe-Zertifikat A2 (for adults). Same four parts, longer and more demanding than A1.

TELC

TELC (The European Language Certificates) exams are more practical in orientation. The texts and scenarios tend to feel more "real life": you might read an actual job ad, a text message from a landlord, or an email about rescheduling an appointment.

  • TELC Deutsch A1 -- similar structure to Goethe but with a slightly different weighting of tasks.
  • TELC Deutsch A2 -- includes a Sprachbausteine section (gap-fill grammar exercises) that Goethe does not have at this level. This is a significant difference.

Which one to pick? If your goal is a visa for Germany (spouse visa, work visa), both are accepted. If the institution you are applying to does not specify, go with whichever has a test center closer to you. If you struggle with grammar gap-fill exercises, Goethe might be slightly easier at A2 since it does not have Sprachbausteine. If you want more practice-oriented content, TELC is a good fit.

Exam Structure: What to Expect on Test Day

Both Goethe and TELC follow the same four-skill structure. Here is what each part looks like at the A1 and A2 level.

Reading (Lesen)

At A1, you read very short texts: signs, notices, simple forms, classified ads. You match information, answer true/false questions, or select the correct option from three choices. At A2, the texts get longer and more varied: short emails, instructions, newspaper snippets, online posts. You need to understand the main point and some details.

The best way to prepare is consistent practice with real exam-format texts. Our A1 reading comprehension exercises and A2 reading comprehension exercises follow the exact same formats you will see on exam day.

Listening (Horen)

At A1, you hear short announcements, phone messages, or very brief conversations -- each played twice. At A2, the recordings are longer and played only once for some tasks. You need to pick out specific information: times, places, prices, reasons.

Study tip: listen to German podcasts for beginners, watch simple YouTube channels in German, and practice with exam audio samples from Goethe or TELC websites.

Writing (Schreiben)

A1 writing is minimal: fill in a form with your personal data, write two or three sentences. A2 writing asks you to write a short email or message (around 40-60 words) responding to a prompt. You need to cover three or four content points.

The secret to A2 writing: address every single point in the prompt. Missing one point costs you marks even if your grammar is perfect.

Speaking (Sprechen)

Both levels test speaking in pairs or small groups. At A1, you introduce yourself, ask and answer questions using prompt cards ("What is your name? Where do you come from? What do you do?"), and make a simple request. At A2, you describe something from your daily life, respond to a partner, and negotiate a simple plan ("Let's meet at 3pm -- does that work for you?").

The speaking part makes people nervous, but it is actually the most forgiving section. Examiners are looking for communication, not perfection. If you can get your message across and react to your partner, you are most of the way there.

Essential Grammar for A1

A1 grammar is a small set of rules, but you need to know them cold. There is no room for "I kind of understand it" when the exam gives you three options and two of them are wrong.

Present Tense (Prasens)

This is the only tense you need at A1. Every verb, every sentence. Learn the regular conjugation pattern first:

PronounEndingExample: machen
ich-eich mache
du-stdu machst
er/sie/es-ter macht
wir-enwir machen
ihr-tihr macht
sie/Sie-ensie machen

Then learn the irregular ones that come up constantly: sein (to be), haben (to have), werden (to become), and the stem-changing verbs like fahren (du fahrst), lesen (du liest), sprechen (du sprichst).

Articles and Gender (der, die, das)

Every German noun has a gender, and you need to learn the article with every new word. There are no reliable shortcuts. Some patterns help (words ending in -ung are always feminine, words ending in -chen are always neuter), but mostly it comes down to memorization.

At A1, you need nominative and accusative. The only article that changes between nominative and accusative is the masculine one: der becomes den, ein becomes einen.

CaseMasculineFeminineNeuterPlural
Nominativeder / eindie / einedas / eindie / --
Accusativeden / einendie / einedas / eindie / --

Basic Word Order

German word order in main clauses follows one unbreakable rule: the conjugated verb is always in position 2. Not position 1, not position 3. Position 2.

  • Ich lerne Deutsch. -- Subject (pos. 1) + verb (pos. 2).
  • Heute lerne ich Deutsch. -- Time expression (pos. 1) + verb (pos. 2) + subject flips behind the verb.
  • Am Montag gehe ich zum Arzt. -- Same pattern. The verb stays in position 2.

This is called Verbzweitstellung (verb-second position), and it is the single most important word order rule at A1. If you get this right, your sentences will sound correct even if other things are slightly off.

Yes/No Questions and W-Questions

For yes/no questions, the verb goes to position 1: Lernst du Deutsch? For W-questions (wer, was, wo, wann, wie, warum), the W-word takes position 1 and the verb stays in position 2: Wo wohnst du?

Essential Grammar for A2

A2 grammar builds directly on A1. You keep everything from before and add three major areas.

Perfekt (Conversational Past Tense)

This is the past tense used in everyday spoken German. It is formed with haben or sein + past participle (Partizip II).

  • Ich habe Deutsch gelernt. -- I learned German. (haben + ge-lern-t)
  • Er ist nach Berlin gefahren. -- He went to Berlin. (sein + ge-fahr-en)

The tricky part is knowing which verbs take sein instead of haben. The rule: verbs that describe a change of location or state (gehen, fahren, kommen, fliegen, werden, sterben) use sein. Everything else uses haben.

You also need to know the irregular past participles. There is no shortcut here: geschrieben (schreiben), getrunken (trinken), gegessen (essen), gesprochen (sprechen). Make flashcards. Drill them.

Modal Verbs (Modalverben)

There are six modal verbs in German, and at A2 you need all of them:

VerbMeaningExample
konnencan / to be able toIch kann Deutsch sprechen.
mussenmust / to have toDu musst zum Arzt gehen.
wollenwant toWir wollen nach Deutschland fahren.
sollenshould / to be supposed toEr soll mehr lernen.
durfenmay / to be allowed toHier darf man nicht rauchen.
mogento likeIch mag Kaffee.

The key thing to remember: when you use a modal verb, the main verb goes to the end of the sentence in its infinitive form. Ich kann gut Deutsch sprechen. This sentence frame (modal in position 2, infinitive at the end) shows up in almost every A2 exam task.

Separable Verbs (Trennbare Verben)

Many German verbs have a prefix that splits off and moves to the end of the sentence: aufstehen, anfangen, einkaufen, mitkommen, zuruckkommen.

  • Ich stehe um 7 Uhr auf. -- I get up at 7. The prefix auf- goes to the end.
  • Wann fangst du an? -- When do you start?
  • Er kauft im Supermarkt ein. -- He shops at the supermarket.

In the Perfekt, the ge- goes between the prefix and the verb stem: aufgestanden, angefangen, eingekauft. This is a classic A2 exam trap.

Basic Subordinate Clauses

At A2, you need to handle three conjunctions that push the verb to the end of the clause: weil (because), dass (that), and wenn (when/if).

  • Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Deutschland arbeiten will.
  • Ich denke, dass er morgen kommt.
  • Ich bleibe zu Hause, wenn es regnet.

The pattern: conjunction + subject + everything else + verb at the very end. Once this clicks, your A2 writing and speaking immediately sound more natural. These conjunctions also appear in Sprachbausteine exercises, so practice them in context.

Vocabulary: What Topics Keep Coming Up?

Exams do not test random vocabulary. They test the same topic areas over and over. If you focus your study on these, you cover 80% of what shows up.

A1 Vocabulary Topics

  • Personal information: name, age, nationality, address, phone number, email, family members.
  • Daily routine: getting up, eating, going to work/school, sleeping.
  • Food and drink: ordering at a restaurant, shopping at a supermarket, basic cooking vocabulary.
  • Home and living: rooms, furniture, describing where you live.
  • Time and dates: days of the week, months, telling time, making appointments.
  • Transport: bus, train, car, buying a ticket, asking for directions.
  • Health basics: body parts, saying what hurts, going to the doctor.

A2 Vocabulary Topics (in addition to A1)

  • Work and profession: job titles, workplace vocabulary, applying for a job, writing a simple CV.
  • Education: school subjects, courses, talking about what you studied.
  • Travel and holidays: booking accommodation, describing trips, at the hotel, at the airport.
  • Weather: describing weather, seasons, clothing for different weather.
  • Media and communication: phone calls, emails, simple internet vocabulary, newspapers.
  • Shopping and services: returning items, complaints, at the bank, at the post office.
  • Feelings and opinions: expressing likes/dislikes, agreement/disagreement, simple opinions.

Practical tip: do not just memorize word lists. Learn words in sentences and practice them with reading exercises. Our A1 Leseverstehen and A2 Leseverstehen sections use exactly these topic areas, so you build vocabulary while training a real exam skill.

8-12 Week Study Timeline

This plan assumes you are studying 45-60 minutes a day, five days a week. Adjust the pace to your schedule, but keep the sequence -- each phase builds on the one before it.

Phase 1: Foundation (Weeks 1-3)

  • Learn the alphabet and pronunciation rules. German pronunciation is very regular once you know the system.
  • Start with personal information vocabulary and the present tense of regular verbs.
  • Memorize sein and haben conjugations perfectly.
  • Practice nominative articles (der/die/das) with every new noun. Say the article out loud every time.
  • Do 2-3 very simple reading exercises per week (A1 level).
  • Learn numbers 1-100, days of the week, months.

Phase 2: Core Grammar (Weeks 4-6)

  • Master the accusative case (den/einen). Practice with sentences, not just tables.
  • Learn 3-5 stem-changing verbs per week: fahren, lesen, sprechen, essen, schlafen, sehen, geben, nehmen.
  • Start building topic vocabulary systematically (food, home, transport).
  • Practice word order: verb in position 2 rule. Write 10 sentences a day rearranging the first element.
  • If targeting A2: start Perfekt with regular verbs (ge- + stem + -t).

Phase 3: Expansion (Weeks 7-9)

  • For A1: consolidate everything. Do full practice tests. Focus on weak areas.
  • For A2: add modal verbs and separable verbs. These are high-frequency and high-reward.
  • Practice writing short emails (A2 writing task format).
  • Work on Sprachbausteine exercises if taking TELC.
  • Start the speaking part: practice self-introduction until it is automatic. Record yourself.
  • Do 4-5 reading exercises per week, mixing A1 and A2 difficulty.

Phase 4: Exam Simulation (Weeks 10-12)

  • Do at least two full practice exams under real conditions: timed, no dictionary, no pauses.
  • Review every mistake from the practice tests. Categorize errors: vocabulary, grammar, reading comprehension, time management.
  • Drill your weakest grammar area daily for 15 minutes.
  • Practice the speaking part with a partner or teacher. A trial lesson focused on exam speaking can make a real difference in the last two weeks.
  • Read through the exam rules and logistics so nothing surprises you on test day.

Study Tips That Actually Work

After years of preparing students for these exams, here are the things that make the biggest difference.

1. Practice with exam-format materials

General German textbooks are fine for learning, but once you start exam prep, switch to materials that match the test format. The tasks in Goethe and TELC exams have specific patterns, and knowing those patterns saves you time and reduces stress. Use our reading comprehension practice tips for a structured approach.

2. Learn grammar in context, not in isolation

Memorizing grammar tables is necessary, but it is not sufficient. You need to see grammar in real sentences and produce it yourself. When you learn the Perfekt, do not just memorize gegessen -- write five sentences about what you ate yesterday. The brain learns grammar through use, not through staring at tables.

3. Focus on high-frequency vocabulary

The A1 and A2 exams test a limited set of topics (see above). Spending time on obscure vocabulary is inefficient. Cover the core topics first, then expand.

4. Train listening every day

Listening is the skill that improves most slowly and needs the most consistent practice. Even 10 minutes a day of focused listening (not background noise) will make a noticeable difference over 8 weeks.

5. Do not skip the speaking practice

Many self-study learners prepare reading, writing, and listening thoroughly but neglect speaking. Then they walk into the exam and freeze. Practice speaking out loud, even if you are alone. Describe your room, narrate your morning routine, answer imaginary exam questions.

6. Review mistakes, do not just move on

Keep a notebook of mistakes. Every time you get something wrong in a practice exercise, write down the correct answer and the rule behind it. Review this notebook once a week. Mistakes are data -- they tell you exactly what to study next.

The Most Common Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

These are the errors I see most often when students prepare for A1 and A2 exams. Knowing about them in advance can save you marks.

Forgetting the verb in position 2.

Gestern ich habe Deutsch gelernt is wrong. Correct: Gestern habe ich Deutsch gelernt. The verb always stays in position 2, no matter what comes first.

Mixing up accusative and nominative.

Ich sehe der Mann is wrong. Sehen needs accusative: Ich sehe den Mann. Only the masculine article changes, but that one change gets tested constantly.

Using Perfekt with the wrong auxiliary.

Ich habe nach Berlin gefahren is wrong. Fahren describes a change of location, so: Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren. When in doubt, ask: "Does this verb describe moving somewhere or changing state?" If yes, use sein.

Forgetting to split separable verbs.

Ich aufstehe um 7 Uhr is wrong. The prefix goes to the end: Ich stehe um 7 Uhr auf.

Putting the infinitive in the wrong place with modal verbs.

Ich kann sprechen gut Deutsch is wrong. The infinitive goes to the end: Ich kann gut Deutsch sprechen.

Not answering all points in the writing task.

The A2 writing task gives you 3-4 content points to cover. Missing even one can drop your score significantly, regardless of how good your grammar is. Read the prompt twice, underline each point, check them off as you write.

Ignoring Sprachbausteine (TELC only).

The Sprachbausteine section tests grammar and vocabulary in context through gap-fill exercises. Many students do not practice this format specifically and lose easy marks. Dedicated Sprachbausteine practice can turn this into one of your strongest sections.
